6.5 Evaluation Procedure
The quantitative evaluation followed a reproducible multi-stage protocol designed to ensure fair comparison between all evaluated retrieval systems. First, a frozen metadata snapshot of the Swiss OGD portal was created and used as the common evaluation corpus. By evaluating every retrieval system on the identical corpus, the influence of changes in the live portal was eliminated and all experiments became fully reproducible.
In addition to the user-centred evaluation, a controlled IR benchmarking procedure was conducted to assess the retrieval effectiveness of the proposed framework. All retrieval systems were evaluated using the same benchmark query collection and a common metadata corpus to ensure fair comparison. For each benchmark query, the top-ranked results produced by the portal search, BM25 baseline, weighted-sum baseline, and the proposed Human-Centered Fuzzy Information Retrieval (HCFIR) system were pooled and duplicate datasets were removed.
The pooled candidate datasets were assessed by the author as a single assessor using a graded relevance scale consisting of 0 (not relevant), 1 (partially relevant), and 2 (highly relevant in the final collapsed scale). The resulting judgments were consolidated in a later finalization pass and written to the authoritative ground-truth file used for evaluation. This protocol supports reproducible pooling and publication of the judgments, but it does not support inter-rater agreement statistics because only one assessor produced the labels.
A benchmark query set representing realistic OGD information needs was then executed against each retrieval system. The evaluated systems included the default portal ranking, a BM25-based retrieval model, a weighted-sum baseline, and the proposed Human-Centered Fuzzy IR framework.
Using the final ground truth, each retrieval system was evaluated with MAP, P@5, nDCG@10, and MRR. Statistical significance was assessed using paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests with Holm?Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. In addition, 95% bootstrap confidence intervals and per-query win/loss analysis were computed to quantify the robustness and practical significance of the observed performance differences.
Finally, the robustness of the proposed fuzzy ranking framework was investigated through sensitivity analysis. Rule-weight perturbations and membership-function sensitivity checks were performed to evaluate the influence of individual fuzzy rules on retrieval effectiveness and ranking stability. This additional analysis ensured that the reported retrieval performance was not dependent on a single parameter configuration but remained stable across reasonable variations of the fuzzy inference system [42, 4].

7.3 Retrieval Effectiveness
This section presents the quantitative evaluation of the proposed HCFIR framework.
The objective is to assess how effectively the proposed fuzzy ranking approach retrieves
relevant datasets from the Swiss OGD portal when compared with alternative retrieval
methods. The evaluation was conducted using the benchmark collection introduced in
Chapter 6, consisting of fifteen representative search queries spanning environmental,
mobility, and cross-domain information needs.
Five retrieval systems were evaluated under identical experimental conditions: the
proposed HCFIR framework, the BM25 lexical baseline, the linear weighted baseline, the
weighted-sum baseline, and the default portal ranking. The final judgment file contains
only the overlapping single-assessor judgments that exist in the frozen corpus snapshot,
so the retrieval comparison is reproducible but intentionally limited to the aligned subset.
Retrieval effectiveness was measured using four widely adopted IR metrics: Mean
Average Precision (MAP), Precision at rank five (P@5), Normalized Discounted Cumu-
lative Gain at rank ten (nDCG@10), and Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR). These metrics
evaluate complementary aspects of retrieval quality, including overall ranking accuracy,
early precision, graded relevance, and the position of the first relevant document within
the ranked result list [25, 17].
To provide a statistically reliable comparison, retrieval performance was analysed at
both the aggregate and query levels. In addition to descriptive performance measures,
the evaluation incorporates confidence intervals, score distributions, and non-parametric
statistical significance testing using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Holm?Bonferroni
correction [8, 15]. These analyses determine whether observed performance differences
represent meaningful improvements rather than random variation across the benchmark
queries.
The following subsections present the quantitative retrieval results. Section 7.3.1 com-
pares the overall performance of all retrieval systems using aggregate evaluation metrics.
This is followed by an analysis of query-level behaviour and statistical significance before
discussing the implications of the observed retrieval performance.
7.3.1 Overall Retrieval Performance
This subsection presents the overall retrieval effectiveness of the five evaluated retrieval
systems using the benchmark collection described in Chapter 6. The proposed HCFIR
framework was compared with four alternative retrieval approaches: the default portal
ranking, the weighted-sum baseline, the linear weighted baseline, and the BM25 lexical
retrieval baseline. All systems were evaluated under identical experimental conditions
using the same set of fifteen benchmark queries and the aligned final judgments.
Retrieval effectiveness was assessed using four widely adopted IR metrics: Mean Av-
erage Precision (MAP), Precision at rank five (P@5), Normalized Discounted Cumulative
Gain at rank ten (nDCG@10), and Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR). Together, these met-
rics provide complementary perspectives on retrieval quality by evaluating overall ranking
accuracy, early precision, graded relevance, and the ranking position of the first relevant
result returned to the user [25, 17]. Table 7.1 summarizes the average retrieval perfor-
mance across all fifteen benchmark queries.

trieval effectiveness while remaining fully interpretable. Because the aligned final judg-
ments are sparse, average values alone do not reveal how consistently each retrieval system
performs across individual information needs. The following subsection therefore examines
the retrieval behaviour for each benchmark query in greater detail before statistical signif-
icance is analysed later in this chapter.
7.3.2 Query-Level Retrieval Performance
While the aggregate evaluation metrics presented in the previous subsection provide an
overall comparison of retrieval effectiveness, they do not reveal how consistently each
retrieval system performs across different information needs. To obtain a more detailed
understanding of system behaviour, retrieval performance was analysed individually for
each of the fifteen benchmark queries. This analysis makes it possible to identify query-
specific strengths and weaknesses that may be obscured by average performance measures.
The per-query results show that only two queries contribute non-zero scores in the aligned
benchmark file: MOB-02 and XD-03. MOB-02 demonstrates that the fuzzy framework,
BM25, the weighted-sum baseline, and the portal can all retrieve a judged relevant item,
while XD-03 shows that the lexical and fuzzy systems can also handle a more textually
specific educational query. All other benchmark queries remain zero because the aligned
judgments do not contain relevant documents that are retrieved by the current frozen
snapshot, which makes ENV-01 a coverage problem rather than a ranking distinction.
The most important interpretation is therefore not that one system dominates on every
query, but that the benchmark is sparse and the query-level signals are concentrated in a
small number of information needs. This makes the per-query table useful as a diagnostic
report rather than as a broad ranking contest.
Although the per-query evaluation highlights differences in retrieval behaviour, it does
not determine whether these differences are statistically meaningful. The following sub-
section therefore applies non-parametric statistical significance testing to assess whether
the observed performance differences between retrieval systems are likely to represent
genuine improvements or simply reflect natural variation across the benchmark queries.
7.3.3 Discussion of Retrieval Effectiveness
The quantitative evaluation demonstrates that the proposed HCFIR framework achieves
competitive retrieval effectiveness across the aligned benchmark while maintaining a
transparent and interpretable ranking process. The fuzzy and BM25 systems tie on the
aggregate metrics, and the portal and weighted-sum baselines form the next closest cluster.
This indicates that integrating fuzzy reasoning into the ranking process does not reduce
retrieval effectiveness on this frozen snapshot, while still providing additional explanation
capabilities that are not offered by a conventional lexical baseline alone.
Statistical analysis performed using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Holm?Bonferroni
correction supports a cautious interpretation: the pairwise differences are small and none of
the corrected comparisons provide strong evidence of a winner on the aligned benchmark.
The principal contribution of the fuzzy framework is therefore its interpretable multi-
criteria reasoning, not a statistically robust aggregate win over the other systems.
The quantitative evaluation demonstrates that the proposed HCFIR framework achieves
retrieval effectiveness that is competitive with the other interpretable retrieval approaches
while providing additional benefits in terms of transparency and explainability. Rather
than attempting to maximise retrieval effectiveness alone, the proposed framework ex-
plicitly balances lexical relevance with metadata completeness, recency, and resource
availability using interpretable fuzzy reasoning, resulting in rankings that remain compa-
rable across the benchmark queries while offering explanations for each ranking decision.

7.8 Discussion of Research Questions
The findings presented throughout this chapter provide evidence to address the research
questions introduced in Chapter 1. By combining quantitative retrieval benchmarking
with qualitative user-centered evaluation, the study offers a comprehensive assessment of
the proposed HCIR framework.
RQ1: How can fuzzy logic be used to effectively retrieve Open Government Datasets within OGD portals?
RQ1: How can fuzzy logic be used to effectively retrieve Open Government Datasets within OGD portals?
The proposed framework demonstrates that fuzzy logic provides an effective mechanism
for integrating multiple human-centered relevance criteria into the ranking process. Un-
like conventional keyword-based retrieval systems that primarily rely on lexical similarity,
the Mamdani fuzzy inference system combines textual relevance with metadata complete-
ness, dataset recency, resource availability, and explainable reasoning. The user study
further indicates that participants appreciated the transparency and interpretability pro-
vided by the fuzzy rule-based ranking process, supporting the use of fuzzy logic as an
effective HCIR technique.
Overall, the quantitative retrieval evaluation demonstrates that integrating multiple
metadata-derived relevance criteria within an interpretable fuzzy inference framework can
achieve retrieval effectiveness that remains competitive with conventional lexical retrieval
approaches while providing additional transparency and explainability. These findings
therefore support the feasibility of HCIR for Swiss OGD without claiming superiority
over established ranking methods.
RQ2: How can multiple metadata-based relevance criteria be integrated into a unified and interpretable ranking mechanism?
The quantitative evaluation demonstrates that multiple relevance criteria can be effectively
integrated through a Mamdani fuzzy inference system. By combining textual similarity,
metadata completeness, dataset recency, and resource availability using explicit IF-THEN
rules, the framework demonstrates that multi-criteria ranking can be achieved while re-
maintaining full interpretability. Unlike traditional weighted-sum approaches or black-box
machine-learning models, the fuzzy ranking mechanism provides clear relationships between
inputs, rules, and outputs. The evaluation results indicate that this integration approach
offers competitive retrieval effectiveness while maintaining transparency regarding how
different relevance factors contribute to ranking decisions.
RQ3: To what extent does a fuzzy logic-based retrieval system support Human-Centered Information Retrieval principles?
The evaluation demonstrates that the proposed framework successfully supports the primary
objectives of HCIR by combining competitive retrieval effectiveness with transparency,
explainability, and user-centered interaction. Although conventional retrieval approaches
achieved higher values for several traditional IR metrics, participants consistently reported
positive perceptions regarding usability, explanation quality, confidence, and overall user
experience. These findings suggest that the principal contribution of the framework lies in
improving user understanding of ranking decisions and supporting exploratory dataset dis-
covery while maintaining practical retrieval effectiveness. The integration of interpretable
fuzzy reasoning with metadata-driven explanations demonstrates concrete support for HCIR
principles, particularly transparency and user understanding.
RQ4: How effective are metadata-driven explanations in improving user understanding and trust in ranking decisions?
The qualitative evaluation indicates that metadata-driven explanations substantially im-
proved participant understanding of retrieval decisions. Users consistently reported that
explanation messages, relevance scores, and metadata summaries enabled them to interpret
why datasets appeared in particular ranking positions and increased their confidence in
the retrieval results. Participants indicated that the explanation panel describing ranking
decisions using familiar metadata attributes (rather than internal model parameters) was
particularly valuable. Although explanation quality ultimately depends on the availability
and consistency of metadata published by OGD providers, the findings demonstrate that
interpretable explanation mechanisms represent an important component of HCIR for
OGD portals. The reported improvements in perceived transparency and user trust should
be interpreted within the context of a formative evaluation involving ten participants. While
the results suggest that explanation mechanisms positively influence user perception,
explanation quality and user trust.

7.9 Limitations
Although the proposed HCIR framework demonstrated encouraging results, several lim-
itations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this research.
The first limitation concerns the construction of the benchmark relevance judgments
used for the quantitative evaluation. The benchmark was reconstructed from the over-
lapping judgments that exist in the frozen corpus snapshot and therefore covers only a
small subset of the intended pooled collection. While this approach provides a practical
and reproducible evaluation framework, it naturally favours retrieval approaches that
retrieve the aligned judged items and it leaves some benchmark queries coverage-limited.
Consequently, conventional IR metrics such as MAP and nDCG@10 may not fully capture
the additional benefits provided by human-centered ranking criteria, including transpar-
ency, explainability, and support for exploratory information seeking [40].
A second limitation relates to the scope of the user-centered evaluation. The forma-
tive questionnaire involved ten Master?s students who were familiar with IR concepts and
digital technologies. Although this participant group was appropriate for the formative
evaluation of a research prototype, it does not fully represent the diverse range of potential
users of Swiss OGD, including policy makers, journalists, researchers, public administra-
tors, and citizens. Future studies should therefore involve a larger and more heterogeneous
participant population to improve the external validity of the findings [14, 32].
The evaluation was also limited to datasets retrieved from a single Swiss OGD portal
snapshot. Although the snapshot provides a realistic and representative environment for
the proposed framework, the reported findings may not directly generalize to other OGD
platforms that employ different metadata standards, retrieval infrastructures, or ranking
strategies. Evaluating the proposed framework on additional national and international
OGD portals would strengthen the generalizability of the proposed approach.
Another limitation concerns the comparison with alternative retrieval methods. The
benchmark focused on conventional keyword-based retrieval and weighted metadata rank-
ing approaches because these represent the retrieval mechanisms currently available within
many OGD portals. More recent retrieval paradigms, including dense retrieval, learning-
to-rank methods, and large language model (LLM)-assisted retrieval, were outside the
scope of this research. Future work could investigate how fuzzy HCIR can complement
these modern retrieval approaches while preserving transparency and explainability [9].
Finally, the proposed framework relies on manually designed fuzzy membership func-
tions and expert-defined inference rules. Although this design improves interpretability
and allows users to understand how ranking decisions are produced, it may require adap-
tation when applied to different application domains or metadata schemas. Future research
could investigate semi-automatic optimization of membership functions while maintain-
ing the interpretability that represents one of the principal strengths of the proposed
framework.
Despite these limitations, the combined quantitative and qualitative evaluation demon-
strates that the proposed framework provides a transparent and explainable approach to
HCIR while maintaining competitive retrieval performance for Swiss OGD.

8.3 Research Questions Revisited
This section revisits the research questions introduced in Chapter 1 and summarizes how they were addressed through the study.
8.3.1 RQ1: How can fuzzy logic be used to improve dataset retrieval within OGD portals?
The findings of this research demonstrate that fuzzy logic provides an effective mechanism for integrating multiple relevance criteria within a transparent HCIR framework. By combining keyword relevance, metadata completeness, dataset recency, resource availability, and explanation generation through a Mamdani fuzzy inference system, the proposed framework extends conventional keyword-based retrieval beyond lexical similarity alone. The quantitative evaluation demonstrated competitive retrieval effectiveness, while the user-centered evaluation showed that participants valued the transparency and interpretability provided by the fuzzy ranking process. Together, these findings indicate that fuzzy logic can effectively enhance OGD retrieval by supporting interpretable multi-criteria decision making while maintaining practical retrieval performance.
8.3.2 RQ2: How can multiple metadata-based relevance criteria be integrated into a unified and interpretable ranking mechanism?
This research addressed the integration of multiple relevance criteria through a Mamdani fuzzy inference engine that combines metadata completeness, keyword relevance, recency, and resource availability using linguistic variables and expert-defined inference rules. Unlike traditional weighted-sum approaches, fuzzy inference naturally models gradual transitions between different relevance levels while preserving interpretability. The developed explanation mechanism further enables users to understand how individual metadata attributes influence ranking decisions, thereby supporting transparent and understandable retrieval.
8.3.3 RQ3: To what extent does a fuzzy logic-based retrieval system support HCIR principles?
The evaluation demonstrates that the proposed framework successfully supports the primary objectives of HCIR by combining competitive retrieval effectiveness with transparency, explainability, and user trust. Although conventional retrieval approaches achieved higher values for several traditional IR metrics, participants consistently reported positive perceptions regarding usability, explanation quality, and confidence in the retrieval results. These findings suggest that the principal contribution of the framework lies in improving user understanding of ranking decisions while maintaining practical retrieval effectiveness.
8.3.4 RQ4: How effective are metadata-driven explanations in improving user understanding and trust in ranking decisions?
The qualitative evaluation indicates that metadata-driven explanations substantially improved participant understanding of retrieval decisions. Users consistently reported that explanation messages, relevance scores, and metadata summaries enabled them to interpret why datasets appeared in particular ranking positions and increased their confidence in the retrieval results. Although explanation quality ultimately depends on the availability and consistency of metadata, the findings demonstrate that interpretable explanation mechanisms represent an important component of HCIR for OGD portals.
8.4 Research Contributions
This research contributes to the field of HCIR by demonstrating how fuzzy logic can be integrated with conventional IR techniques to improve the discoverability and interpretability of Swiss OGD. The principal contributions of this thesis are summarized as follows.
1. A HCIR framework for Open Government Data.
This thesis proposes a HCIR framework that extends traditional keyword-based retrieval by integrating multiple metadata-based relevance criteria within an interpretable fuzzy inference process. Unlike conventional retrieval approaches that primarily emphasize lexical similarity, the proposed framework explicitly considers transparency, explainability, metadata quality, and user understanding during ranking.
2. A custom fuzzy multi-criteria ranking mechanism.
A Mamdani fuzzy inference engine was designed and implemented to combine keyword relevance, metadata completeness, dataset recency, and resource availability into a unified ranking score. The rule-based design allows gradual reasoning under uncertainty while maintaining full interpretability of ranking decisions.

Rather than presenting opaque ranking scores, the system explains retrieval decisions using interpretable factors such as keyword matching, metadata completeness, publication recency, and resource availability, thereby supporting user understanding and trust.
4. Integration with the live Swiss OGD portal.
Unlike many conceptual HCIR studies, the proposed framework was implemented as a functional prototype connected to the live CKAN API of the Swiss OGD portal. The system retrieves real datasets and demonstrates the practical feasibility of transparent and explainable retrieval within an operational OGD environment.

6.5 Evaluation Procedure
The quantitative evaluation followed a reproducible multi-stage protocol designed to en-
sure fair comparison between all evaluated retrieval systems. First, a frozen metadata
snapshot of the Swiss OGD portal was created and used as the common evaluation cor-
68

--- PAGE 70 ---
pus. By evaluating every retrieval system on the identical corpus, the influence of changes
in the live portal was eliminated and all experiments became fully reproducible.
In addition to the user-centred evaluation, a controlled IR benchmarking procedure
was conducted to assess the retrieval effectiveness of the proposed framework. All re-
trieval systems were evaluated using the same benchmark query collection and a common
metadata corpus to ensure fair comparison. For each benchmark query, the top-ranked
results produced by the portal search, BM25 baseline, weighted-sum baseline, and the
proposed Human-Centered Fuzzy Information Retrieval (HCFIR) system were pooled and
duplicate datasets were removed.
The pooled candidate datasets were assessed by the author as a single assessor using a
graded relevance scale consisting of 0 (not relevant), 1 (partially relevant), and 2 (highly
relevant in the final collapsed scale). The resulting judgments were consolidated in a later
finalization pass and written to the authoritative ground-truth file used for evaluation.
This protocol supports reproducible pooling and publication of the judgments, but it does
not support inter-rater agreement statistics because only one assessor produced the labels.
A benchmark query set representing realistic OGD information needs was then exe-
cuted against each retrieval system. The evaluated systems included the default portal
ranking, a BM25-based retrieval model, a weighted-sum baseline, and the proposed
Human-Centered Fuzzy IR framework. For every query, the highest-ranked documents
returned by all systems were pooled and duplicate datasets were removed before relevance
assessment.
Using the final ground truth, each retrieval system was evaluated with MAP, P@5,
nDCG@10, and MRR. Statistical significance was assessed using paired Wilcoxon signed-
rank tests with Holm–Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons. In addition, 95%
bootstrap confidence intervals and per-query win/loss analysis were computed to quantify
the robustness and practical significance of the observed performance differences.
Finally, the robustness of the proposed fuzzy ranking framework was investigated
through sensitivity analysis. Rule-weight perturbations and membership-function sensi-
tivity checks were performed to evaluate the influence of individual fuzzy rules on retrieval
effectiveness and ranking stability. This additional analysis ensured that the reported re-
trieval performance was not dependent on a single parameter configuration but remained
stable across reasonable variations of the fuzzy inference system [42, 4].

2.4 Information Retrieval Models
IR systems are designed to identify and rank documents according to their relevance to
user information needs. Within OGD environments, retrieval systems play an important
role in helping users locate datasets from large and heterogeneous repositories. Effective
retrieval models must therefore support accurate relevance estimation while remaining
computationally efficient and interpretable.
Over the years, numerous retrieval approaches have been proposed, ranging from
traditional lexical matching methods to modern semantic retrieval techniques based on
deep learning and language models. Understanding the strengths and limitations of
these approaches is important for selecting an appropriate retrieval foundation for HCIR
systems.
2.4.1 Traditional Information Retrieval Models
Traditional IR approaches estimate relevance primarily through lexical matching between
query terms and document content. These methods assume that documents containing
terms similar to those used in a query are more likely to satisfy user information needs.
One of the earliest and most influential approaches is Term Frequency–Inverse Doc-
ument Frequency (TF-IDF), which assigns weights to terms based on their frequency
within a document and their rarity across the collection [35]. Although TF-IDF remains
widely used, it does not explicitly account for document length normalization or term
frequency saturation.
To address these limitations, probabilistic retrieval models such as BM25 were de-
veloped. BM25 extends traditional term-weighting approaches by incorporating inverse
document frequency, term frequency saturation, and document length normalization. As
a result, BM25 has become one of the most widely adopted retrieval models in both
academic research and real-world search systems [34].

2.5 HCIR
Traditional IR research has primarily focused on improving ranking effectiveness and re-
trieval accuracy. While these objectives remain important, modern retrieval systems must
also consider how users interact with information systems, interpret retrieval outcomes,
and make decisions based on retrieved information.
HCIR extends traditional retrieval approaches by placing users at the center of the
22

--- PAGE 24 ---
retrieval process. Rather than treating retrieval as a purely algorithmic task, HCIR
emphasizes interaction, transparency, interpretability, and support for human decision-
making. The objective is not only to retrieve relevant information but also to help users
understand, explore, and effectively utilize retrieval results.
2.5.1 Principles of HCIR
HCIRisfoundedontherecognitionthatinformationseekingisaninteractiveandevolving
process [36, 26]. Users frequently refine queries, explore alternative information sources,
and modify search objectives as their understanding develops. Consequently, retrieval
systems should support exploration and learning rather than merely returning ranked
lists of documents.
Several principles characterize HCIR. First, retrieval systems should support user in-
teraction by enabling exploration, query refinement, and iterative search behavior. Sec-
ond, systems should provide meaningful feedback that helps users understand search
results and navigate information spaces effectively. Third, retrieval mechanisms should
accommodate uncertainty because users often begin searches with incomplete or evolving
information needs.
Unlike purely system-centered approaches, HCIR recognizes that successful retrieval
dependsnotonlyonalgorithmicperformancebutalsoonuserunderstanding, satisfaction,
and trust. These principles are particularly relevant within OGD environments where
users frequently engage in exploratory search activities and may possess varying levels of
technical expertise.
2.5.2 Transparency and Explainability in HCIR
Transparency and explainability represent fundamental objectives of HCIR [36]. Users
are more likely to trust retrieval systems when they can understand how ranking decisions
are generated and why particular results are recommended.
Traditional retrieval systems often provide limited insight into the reasoning process
underlying ranking outcomes. As a result, users may find it difficult to evaluate the
reliability or relevance of retrieved information. Explainable retrieval systems address
this challenge by providing understandable descriptions of ranking decisions and relevance
estimation processes.
Within HCIR, explainability supports several important objectives. First, it improves
user understanding by making retrieval processes more accessible. Second, it enhances
trust by allowing users to assess the reasoning behind recommendations. Third, it sup-
ports learning and exploration by helping users understand relationships between queries,
metadata attributes, and retrieval outcomes.

Consequently, transparency and explainability are increasingly recognized as essential
characteristics of user-centered retrieval systems.
2.5.3 HCIR in Open Government Data Environments
The principles of HCIR are particularly relevant within OGD environments [21]. Users
of OGD portals often possess diverse backgrounds, varying levels of technical expertise,
and different information needs. Furthermore, many users engage in exploratory search
activities and may be unfamiliar with available datasets or domain-specific terminology.
Traditional keyword-based retrieval systems may struggle to support these complex
information-seeking behaviors because they focus primarily on lexical matching rather
than user understanding. HCIR approaches seek to address these limitations by empha-
sizing interaction, transparency, explainability, and support for exploratory search.
In the context of this research, HCIR provides the conceptual foundation for the devel-
opment of an explainable retrieval framework that combines BM25 retrieval, fuzzy logic-
based ranking, and metadata-driven explanations. By integrating these components, the
proposed framework aims to improve both retrieval effectiveness and user understanding
within the Swiss OGD portal.
2.6 Fuzzy Logic and Information Retrieval
Traditional retrieval systems frequently represent relevance as a binary concept, where
documents are considered either relevant or non-relevant. However, human judgments
rarely operate in such a strictly categorical manner. Users often perceive relevance as
gradual, uncertain, and context-dependent. Consequently, alternative approaches are re-
quired to model the ambiguity and subjectivity inherent in information-seeking behavior.
Fuzzy logic provides a mathematical framework for representing uncertainty and grad-
ual reasoning. Introduced by Zadeh [42], fuzzy logic allows concepts to be represented
through varying degrees of membership rather than binary classifications. This capabil-
ity makes fuzzy logic particularly attractive for IR applications where relevance is often
subjective and difficult to define precisely.
2.6.1 Fundamentals of Fuzzy Logic
Unlike classical Boolean logic, which assigns elements exclusively to either membership or
non-membership categories, fuzzy logic allows partial membership. Membership values
typically range between 0 and 1, representing the degree to which an element belongs to
a particular fuzzy set [42].
Fuzzy logic utilizes linguistic variables such as Low, Medium, and High to describe
concepts that cannot be defined precisely. Membership functions are used to map nu-
24

--- PAGE 26 ---
merical values into these linguistic categories, thereby enabling gradual reasoning and the
representation of uncertainty [42].
This flexibility allows fuzzy systems to model complex human judgments more nat-
urally than traditional binary approaches. Consequently, fuzzy logic has been widely
applied in decision support systems, expert systems, control systems, and IR applica-
tions.
2.6.2 Fuzzy Inference Systems
A fuzzy inference system combines fuzzy sets, linguistic variables, and rule-based reason-
ing to generate decisions or relevance estimates. The process typically consists of four
stages: fuzzification, rule evaluation, aggregation, and defuzzification.
Among the available inference approaches, the Mamdani model is one of the most
widely used because of its interpretability and compatibility with human reasoning [24].
Mamdani systems employ IF–THEN rules that describe relationships between input vari-
ables and output decisions using linguistic terms.
For example:
IF Similarity is High AND Metadata Completeness is High THEN Relevance
is Very High.
Such rules resemble human decision-making processes and therefore contribute to
transparency and explainability.
2.6.3 Fuzzy Logic in Information Retrieval
Fuzzy logic has been applied extensively within IR research to address limitations asso-
ciated with binary relevance judgments [4]. Rather than classifying documents as simply
relevant or non-relevant, fuzzy retrieval approaches represent relevance as a gradual con-
cept characterized by varying degrees of membership.
Several studies have demonstrated that fuzzy logic can improve retrieval performance
by integrating multiple relevance criteria and accommodating uncertainty within user
information needs. Fuzzy approaches are particularly useful in environments where rele-
vance depends on multiple factors rather than a single similarity measure.
Within OGD repositories, dataset relevance may depend not only on textual simi-
larity but also on metadata quality, recency, completeness, and contextual suitability.
Fuzzy inference systems provide a natural mechanism for combining these heterogeneous
relevance indicators into a unified ranking framework.

2.6.4 Why Fuzzy Logic Supports HCIR
One of the central arguments of this thesis is that fuzzy logic provides characteristics that
align closely with HCIR principles.
First, fuzzy logic models gradual relevance rather than binary relevance [4]. Human
users rarely perceive information as completely relevant or completely irrelevant. Instead,
relevance is often evaluated along a continuum influenced by context, task requirements,
and personal preferences. Fuzzy logic naturally supports this type of reasoning.
Second, fuzzy inference systems accommodate uncertainty and ambiguity [42]. Users
frequently submit incomplete, vague, or exploratory queries, particularly within OGD
environments. Fuzzy reasoning enables retrieval systems to handle such uncertainty more
effectively than rigid rule-based approaches.
Third, fuzzy logic promotes transparency and explainability through explicit IF–
THEN rules [36]. Unlike many black-box machine learning models, fuzzy systems allow
users to understand how relevance estimates are generated and which factors contribute
to ranking decisions.
Finally, fuzzy logic facilitates the integration of multiple relevance indicators. In the
proposed framework, thematic similarity, metadata completeness, and dataset recency
are combined through fuzzy reasoning to generate relevance estimates that more closely
reflect human judgment.
For these reasons, fuzzy logic represents a suitable foundation for HCIR systems that
emphasize transparency, interpretability, and user understanding.
From a HCIR perspective, fuzzy logic enables retrieval systems to operate in a manner
thatmorecloselyresembleshumanreasoning. Humanusersoftenevaluaterelevanceusing
imprecise concepts such as "mostly relevant", "somewhat useful", or "highly related"
rather than strict numerical thresholds. Fuzzy logic allows these gradual judgments to
be represented mathematically through linguistic variables and membership functions.
Consequently, fuzzy reasoning provides a natural bridge between computational ranking
mechanisms and human relevance perception, making it particularly suitable for HCIR
environments [3, 42].
While alternative approaches such as weighted-sum ranking, learning-to-rank models,
and multi-criteria decision-making methods (e.g., AHP or TOPSIS) can also combine
multiple relevance factors, they present different trade-offs. Weighted-sum approaches
assume fixed linear relationships between ranking criteria and cannot naturally represent
gradual transitions between linguistic concepts such aslow,medium, andhighrelevance.
Learning-to-rank methods generally require substantially larger collections of labelled
training data than were available in this study and often reduce transparency by produc-
ing complex learned ranking functions. Similarly, multi-criteria decision-making methods
are well suited for structured decision problems but typically provide less flexibility for

modelling uncertainty and subjective relevance judgments. Fuzzy inference was therefore
selected because it supports interpretable rule-based reasoning while allowing multiple
metadata-derived relevance criteria to be combined in a manner that closely reflects hu-
man reasoning under uncertainty.
Among the available fuzzy inference approaches, the Mamdani model was selected in
preferencetoSugenoorTakagi–Sugeno–Kang(TSK)inferencebecausetheprimaryobjec-
tive of this research is interpretability rather than computational optimisation. Mamdani
inference produces linguistic output membership functions that can be directly trans-

ability within HCIR. Although Sugeno-based systems are computationally efficient and
are often preferred for optimisation and control applications, their numerical outputs
are generally less intuitive for explaining ranking decisions to end users. Consequently,
Mamdani inference provides a more appropriate foundation for the proposed explainable
retrieval framework [38, 42].
2.7 Explainable Information Retrieval
As retrieval systems become increasingly complex, users often find it difficult to un-
derstand why particular documents or datasets are recommended. Although advanced
ranking algorithms may improve retrieval effectiveness, a lack of transparency can reduce
user trust and hinder effective decision-making. Consequently, explainability has emerged
as an important research area within IR.
Explainable Information Retrieval (XIR) seeks to provide users with understandable
descriptions of ranking decisions and retrieval outcomes. Rather than presenting search
results as opaque recommendations, explainable systems communicate the factors that
influenced relevance estimation and document ranking. This transparency helps users
interpret retrieval results and assess their suitability for specific information needs.
2.7.1 Explainability in Information Retrieval
Explainability refers to the ability of a system to communicate the reasoning process
underlying its decisions in a manner that is understandable to human users [27]. Within
IR, explainability aims to clarify why particular documents are retrieved, how relevance
scores are generated, and which factors contribute to ranking decisions.
Explainable retrieval systems can provide information regarding query-document sim-
ilarity, metadata characteristics, relevance indicators, and ranking criteria. Such expla-
nations help users understand retrieval outcomes and improve confidence in system rec-
ommendations.
27

--- PAGE 29 ---
2.7.2 Benefits and Challenges of Explainability
Explainability provides several important benefits within retrieval systems [27]. First, it
improves transparency by exposing the factors that influence ranking decisions. Second,
it supports user trust by enabling users to understand and evaluate retrieval outcomes.
Third, explanations can facilitate learning and exploratory search by helping users un-
derstand relationships between queries and retrieved information.
Despite these advantages, implementing explainability presents several challenges.
Explanations must remain accurate while also being understandable to users with varying
levels of expertise. Excessively technical explanations may confuse users, whereas overly
simplified explanations may fail to communicate meaningful information.
Designing effective explanations therefore requires balancing transparency, usability,
and informational value. HCIR emphasizes this balance by focusing on explanations that
support user understanding and decision-making.
2.7.3 Explainability for Open Government Data Retrieval
Explainability is particularly valuable within OGD environments because users often
possess limited knowledge regarding available datasets, metadata structures, and retrieval
mechanisms. Consequently, users may find it difficult to determine why specific datasets
are recommended or how relevance scores are generated.
Providing explanations alongside retrieval results can improve user understanding of
dataset characteristics and assist users in evaluating dataset suitability [27]. Metadata-
driven explanations are especially useful because they communicate observable factors
such as thematic similarity, metadata completeness, and dataset recency.
In the proposed framework, explainability is achieved through the integration of fuzzy
logic-based reasoning and metadata-driven relevance estimation. The use of explicit fuzzy
rules enables ranking decisions to be communicated in a transparent and understandable
manner, thereby supporting HCIR objectives and improving user trust in retrieval out-
comes.
Unlike post-hoc explanation methods that generate explanations independently of the
underlying ranking process, the proposed framework producesfaithfulexplanations that
directly reflect the reasoning performed by the fuzzy inference system. The generated
explanations are derived from the same metadata features, fuzzy membership functions,
and rule activations used to compute the final relevance score. Consequently, the explana-
tion presented to the user corresponds directly to the actual ranking process rather than
providing a separate or approximate interpretation. This close correspondence between
ranking and explanation supports transparency, accountability, and user trust, which are
fundamental objectives of HCIR and Explainable IR.

TheliteraturereviewedinthischapterdemonstratessignificantprogressinOGDretrieval,

remain that motivate the development of the proposed framework.
Although existing studies provide valuable contributions, relatively few approaches

2.8.1 Research Gap
The literature reveals several limitations in current OGD retrieval systems.
First, many OGD portals rely primarily on traditional keyword-based retrieval mecha-
nisms. While such approaches can efficiently identify datasets containing matching query
terms, they often struggle to accommodate exploratory search behavior, uncertainty, and
evolving information needs. Consequently, retrieval effectiveness may be limited when
users are unfamiliar with available datasets or employ vocabulary different from that
used in metadata descriptions.
Second, existing retrieval approaches frequently focus on textual similarity while over-
looking additional factors that may influence dataset relevance. Characteristics such as
metadata completeness, dataset recency, and metadata quality can affect the usefulness
of datasets but are often excluded from ranking processes.
Third, explainability remains limited within many retrieval environments. Users are
typically presented with ranked results without receiving meaningful explanations regard-
ing how relevance scores were generated. This lack of transparency can reduce trust and
make it difficult for users to evaluate retrieval outcomes.
Finally, despite increasing interest in HCIR, relatively little research has investigated
the integration of HCIR principles, fuzzy logic-based reasoning, and OGD retrieval within
a unified framework. Existing studies tend to focus on individual aspects of retrieval
rather than providing a comprehensive solution that combines retrieval effectiveness,
transparency, explainability, and user-centered interaction.
These limitations highlight the need for retrieval frameworks capable of supporting
both effective dataset discovery and meaningful user understanding.
More specifically, no identified study was found that combines BM25 retrieval, meta-
data completeness assessment, dataset recency evaluation, fuzzy logic-based relevance es-
timation, and explainable HCIR within the context of the Swiss OGD portal [34, 4, 36].

5.7 Custom Mamdani Fuzzy Inference Engine
The fuzzy ranking component was implemented using a custom Mamdani fuzzy inference

and explainability generation.
The implementation follows the Mamdani fuzzy inference approach, one of the most
widely adopted fuzzy reasoning models due to its interpretability and ability to represent
human decision-making processes through linguistic rules [24, 42]. This characteristic

The fuzzy inference engine consists of four primary stages:
1. Fuzzification
2. Rule Evaluation
3. Aggregation
4. Defuzzification

predefined membership functions. Rule evaluation applies the fuzzy rule base to deter-
mine the degree of activation for each rule. Aggregation combines the outputs of all
activated rules, while defuzzification converts the aggregated fuzzy output into a single
numerical relevance score.

Justification for Selecting the Mamdani Fuzzy Inference System
The selection of the Mamdani fuzzy inference system was guided primarily by the objec-
tive of supporting HCIR through transparent and interpretable ranking decisions. Unlike
Takagi–Sugeno (TSK) fuzzy systems, which generate mathematical output functions,
Mamdani inference represents both inputs and outputs using linguistic variables such as
Low,Medium, andHigh. This linguistic representation enables ranking decisions to be
communicated in a manner that is understandable to end users.
Since one of the primary objectives of this research is to improve transparency and
explainability, interpretability was considered more important than computational effi-
ciency. The linguistic outputs generated by the Mamdani approach integrate naturally
with the explanation component of the proposed framework, allowing users to understand
how metadata completeness, keyword relevance, publication recency, and organizational
quality jointly influence dataset ranking.

Although Takagi–Sugeno fuzzy systems are widely used in control and optimization
applications because of their computational efficiency, the Mamdani approach is gener-
ally considered more suitable for decision-support systems in which human interpretation
of inference results is required. Consequently, the Mamdani fuzzy inference system was
selected as the most appropriate reasoning mechanism for the proposed HCIR frame-
work [42, 38, 13].
Why Learning-to-Rank Was Not Adopted
Learning-to-Rank (LTR) approaches represent an important class of IR algorithms that
learn ranking functions directly from labelled training data. Although these techniques
have demonstrated strong retrieval performance in many large-scale retrieval tasks, they
were not adopted in this research for several reasons.
First, the benchmark developed for this study contains a relatively small number
of representative queries and manually reviewed relevance judgments, which is insuffi-
cient for training and validating a supervised Learning-to-Rank model. Second, many
Learning-to-Rank algorithms operate as complex machine learning models whose rank-
ing decisions are difficult to interpret. Since the objective of this research is to support
HCIR through transparent and explainable ranking, interpretability was considered a
primary design requirement.
Consequently, a rule-based Mamdani fuzzy inference system was selected because it
enables explicit modelling of multiple metadata-based relevance criteria while preserving
transparencyandallowinguserstounderstandhowrankingdecisionsaregenerated[42,9].
Designrationale.Theproposedfuzzyinferencesystemcombinesdata-calibratedmem-
bership functions with an expert-defined rule base because these two components rep-
resent different types of knowledge. Membership functions describe observable charac-
teristics of the metadata, such as similarity scores, metadata completeness, recency, and
resource availability. Since these variables are directly measurable from the dataset col-
lection, their breakpoints were calibrated using the statistical distribution of the corpus to
ensure that thelinguisticcategories accurately reflected the characteristics ofthe available
metadata.
In contrast, the fuzzy rules represent human judgement regarding how different rel-
evance factors should be combined during ranking. Such preferences cannot be derived
reliably from the relatively small benchmark query set used in this study and therefore re-
quire expert knowledge. Defining the rules manually also preserves transparency because
every ranking decision can be traced back to explicit IF–THEN rules that are under-
standable to both developers and users. This combination of data-driven calibration and
expert knowledge provides a balance between empirical adaptation and interpretability,
consistent with the objectives of HCIR.

5.7.1 Fuzzy Ranking Inputs
The proposed fuzzy ranking framework utilizes four complementary relevance indicators
to evaluate resource availability and relevance. These indicators were selected to address
limitations commonly observed in traditional retrieval systems that rely exclusively on

The first input isThematic Similarity, which represents the degree of correspon-

The second input isMetadata Completeness. This indicator evaluates the extent
to which important metadata attributes are available for a dataset. Datasets containing
complete and well-structured metadata generally provide greater contextual information
and are easier for users to interpret and assess. Previous studies have identified meta-
data quality as a critical factor influencing dataset discoverability and reuse within OGD
environments [21, 6, 28].
The third input isDataset Recency. Recency measures the freshness of a dataset
based on available publication and update information. In many OGD scenarios, users
prefer datasets that have been recently updated because they are more likely to reflect
current conditions and provide reliable information. Incorporating recency therefore en-
ables the ranking framework to account for temporal relevance in addition to thematic
relevance [16, 1].
The fourth input isResource Availability. This indicator evaluates the practical
accessibility and usability of a dataset by considering the availability of associated re-
sources, such as downloadable files or supporting data services. Even when a dataset is
topically relevant, limited resource availability may reduce its usefulness for users wishing
to inspect or reuse the data. Incorporating this criterion therefore extends the ranking
process beyond topical relevance by accounting for the practical utility of search results,
an important consideration in HCIR and OGD environments [1, 3].
Before fuzzy inference is performed, each input variable is transformed into fuzzy
linguistic variables through the fuzzification process. This transformation allows quanti-
tative measurements to be represented using human-interpretable concepts such asLow,
Medium, andHigh. The resulting fuzzy representations provide the foundation for rule-

By combining thematic similarity, metadata completeness, dataset recency, and re-
source availability, the proposed framework evaluates dataset relevance from multiple

5.7.2 Explanation Generation

descriptions of the reasoning process underlying relevance estimation [27, 3].
The explanation generation component was designed to transform fuzzy reasoning

evance scores, the system identifies the primary factors contributing to dataset rankings
and communicates these factors through concise textual explanations.
Explanations are generated directly from the outputs of the ranking process rather
than from predefined textual templates. For each ranked dataset, the system identifies
the relevance indicators that contributed most strongly to the final score, including the-
matic similarity, metadata completeness, dataset recency, and resource availability. These
values are combined with information derived from the fuzzy inference process to produce
faithful explanations that accurately reflect the factors influencing the ranking decision.
Consequently, the explanation component communicates the actual reasoning underly-
ing the computed relevance score rather than presenting a generic post-hoc justification,
thereby improving transparency and user trust [27, 9, 3].
The explanation generation process follows three primary steps:
1. Identification of dominant ranking factors.
2. Analysis of fuzzy inference outcomes.
3. Generation of human-readable explanations.
This approach enables users to understand how different relevance indicators con-
tributed to ranking decisions. By exposing the reasoning process, the system promotes
transparency and supports informed decision-making during exploratory search activities.
The explanation module is tightly integrated with the ranking pipeline rather than op-
erating as an independent post-processing component. After the fuzzy inference engine

5.11 Implementation Challenges
ThedevelopmentoftheproposedHCIRframeworkinvolvedseveraltechnicalandmethod-

model implementation, fuzzy system design, and explainability generation. Addressing
these challenges was necessary to ensure the reliability, effectiveness, and usability of the
final prototype.
5.11.1 Metadata Quality and Consistency
One of the primary challenges involved the quality and consistency of metadata available
through the Swiss OGD portal. Dataset publishers often provide metadata with varying
levels of completeness, detail, and standardization. Some datasets contained comprehen-
sive metadata descriptions, while others lacked important attributes such as tags, groups,
or detailed descriptions.
Thisinconsistencycomplicatedbothretrievalandrankingprocessesbecausemetadata
quality directly influences dataset discoverability. To mitigate this issue, preprocessing
and validation procedures were incorporated into the acquisition pipeline to improve
metadata handling and ensure that incomplete records could still be processed effectively
[1, 21, 28].
5.11.2 Balancing Multiple Relevance Factors
Traditional retrieval systems typically rely on textual similarity as the primary ranking
criterion. In contrast, the proposed framework integrates thematic similarity, metadata
completeness, and dataset recency and resource availability. Determining how these fac-
tors should interact during relevance estimation represented a significant design challenge.
The fuzzy inference approach was selected to address this issue because it provides
a flexible mechanism for combining multiple relevance indicators while preserving in-
terpretability. Designing an appropriate rule base required iterative refinement to en-
sure that ranking outcomes reflected realistic user expectations and relevance judgments
[42, 24, 4].

5.11.3 Design of Membership Functions
Another challenge involved the definition of membership functions used during fuzzifica-
tion. Membership functions determine how numerical inputs are transformed into lin-
guistic concepts such asLow,Medium, andHigh. Poorly defined membership functions
can lead to unrealistic relevance estimations and unstable ranking behavior.
Several membership function configurations were evaluated during development to
identify representations that produced meaningful and interpretable ranking outcomes.
The final configuration was selected based on consistency, interpretability, and compati-
bility with the retrieval objectives of the framework [43, 42].
5.11.4 Explainability and User Understanding
Providing meaningful explanations for ranking decisions presented an additional chal-
lenge. While numerical relevance scores can indicate dataset importance, they do not
necessarily help users understand why particular datasets were ranked highly.
To address this limitation, the explanation generation component was designed to
identify dominant ranking factors and translate them into human-readable descriptions.
This approach improves transparency and aligns with HCIR principles that emphasize
user understanding and trust [27, 3].
5.11.5 System Integration
Integrating multiple components into a unified retrieval framework also required careful
coordination. Metadata acquisition, BM25 retrieval, similarity normalization, fuzzy in-
ference, explanation generation, and UI components needed to communicate efficiently
while maintaining consistent data representations.
Ensuring compatibility among these components required iterative testing and re-
finement throughout the development process. Successful integration was essential for
achieving the overall objective of providing an effective and explainable dataset retrieval
environment.
Despite these challenges, the final implementation successfully integrated all major
components into a functional prototype capable of supporting HCIR for Swiss OGD.

6.7 Evaluation Metrics
Retrieval effectiveness was evaluated using multiple complementary IR metrics to capture
different aspects of ranking quality. Rather than relying on a single performance mea-
sure, the evaluation employed Mean Average Precision (MAP), Precision at five (P@5),
Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain at rank ten (nDCG@10), and Mean Reciprocal
Rank (MRR). Together, these metrics provide a comprehensive assessment of retrieval
performance from both system-oriented and user-oriented perspectives [25, 17].
Mean Average Precision (MAP) measures the overall quality of the ranked result list
by averaging the precision obtained whenever a relevant document is retrieved. MAP
rewards retrieval systems that consistently rank relevant datasets near the top of the
result list and is widely regarded as one of the standard evaluation measures in IR research
[25].
Precision at five (P@5) evaluates the proportion of relevant datasets among the first
five retrieved results. Since users typically examine only the highest-ranked search results,
P@5 provides an estimate of the practical usefulness of the ranking for real-world search
scenarios [2].
Normalized Discounted Cumulative Gain at rank ten (nDCG@10) evaluates ranking
quality while accounting for graded relevance judgments and the position of relevant
datasetswithintherankedlist. Highlyrelevantdatasetsappearingnearthetopcontribute
more strongly to the final score than equally relevant datasets appearing lower in the
ranking, making nDCG particularly appropriate for graded relevance evaluation [17].
Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR) measures how quickly the first relevant dataset appears
in the ranked results by computing the reciprocal of its rank position. This metric reflects
how efficiently users can locate useful information during exploratory dataset search [39].
To evaluate whether observed performance differences between retrieval systems were
statistically meaningful, paired Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were performed on the per-
query evaluation results. Holm–Bonferroni correction was subsequently applied to adjust
for multiple pairwise comparisons and to control the family-wise error rate [41, 15].
In addition, 95% bootstrap confidence intervals were estimated using query-level resam-
pling, and win/loss analysis was conducted to compare retrieval systems on individual
benchmark queries. Together, these complementary analyses provide stronger evidence
than aggregate metric values alone by quantifying both statistical significance and the
robustness of the observed performance differences.
Although the retrieval metrics quantify different aspects of ranking quality, they do
not by themselves indicate whether observed differences between retrieval systems are
statistically meaningful. Therefore, the metric values reported in Chapter 7 are comple-
mented by non-parametric statistical analyses, including the Wilcoxon signed-rank test,
Holm–Bonferroni correction for multiple comparisons, and bootstrap confidence inter-

assumptions cannot be guaranteed. Holm–Bonferroni correction was applied to control
the family-wise error rate when multiple pairwise comparisons were conducted. In ad-
dition, bootstrap confidence intervals and win–loss analyses were used to evaluate the
consistency of observed improvements across the benchmark query set. These statistical
procedures reduce the risk of interpreting random performance variations as meaningful
improvements and provide stronger evidence for observed ranking differences [8].
Beyond quantitative benchmarking, a user-centered evaluation was conducted with
ten Master’s students to examine usability, perceived retrieval quality, transparency
of ranking explanations, and overall user satisfaction. Participants completed realis-
tic search tasks using the prototype before answering a structured questionnaire. The
qualitative findings complement the quantitative retrieval metrics by assessing whether
improvements in ranking effectiveness also translate into a better search experience, con-
sistent with HCIR principles [12, 29].
Finally, the evaluation investigated the contribution of individual fuzzy logic compo-
nents through sensitivity analysis. By systematically varying the influence of the fuzzy
ranking factors, the study examined how each component affected retrieval effectiveness
and overall ranking stability. This analysis provides additional evidence regarding the
robustness of the proposed ranking model and identifies which fuzzy features contribute
most strongly to retrieval performance.

7.3 Retrieval Effectiveness
This section presents the quantitative evaluation of the proposed HCFIR framework.
The objective is to assess how effectively the proposed fuzzy ranking approach retrieves
relevant datasets from the Swiss OGD portal when compared with alternative retrieval
methods. The evaluation was conducted using the benchmark collection introduced in
Chapter 6, consisting of fifteen representative search queries spanning environmental,
mobility, and cross-domain information needs.
Five retrieval systems were evaluated under identical experimental conditions: the
proposed HCFIR framework, the BM25 lexical baseline, the linear weighted baseline, the
weighted-sum baseline, and the default portal ranking. The final judgment file contains
only the overlapping single-assessor judgments that exist in the frozen corpus snapshot,
so the retrieval comparison is reproducible but intentionally limited to the aligned subset.
Retrieval effectiveness was measured using four widely adopted IR metrics: Mean
Average Precision (MAP), Precision at rank five (P@5), Normalized Discounted Cumu-
lative Gain at rank ten (nDCG@10), and Mean Reciprocal Rank (MRR). These metrics
evaluate complementary aspects of retrieval quality, including overall ranking accuracy,
early precision, graded relevance, and the position of the first relevant document within
the ranked result list [25, 17].
To provide a statistically reliable comparison, retrieval performance was analysed at
both the aggregate and query levels. In addition to descriptive performance measures,
the evaluation incorporates confidence intervals, score distributions, and non-parametric
statistical significance testing using the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Holm?Bonferroni
correction [8, 15]. These analyses determine whether observed performance differences
represent meaningful improvements rather than random variation across the benchmark
queries.
The following subsections present the quantitative retrieval results. Section 7.3.1 com-
pares the overall performance of all retrieval systems using aggregate evaluation metrics.
This is followed by an analysis of query-level behaviour and statistical significance before
discussing the implications of the observed retrieval performance.

7.5 Transparency and Explainability
One of the primary objectives of the proposed HCIR framework is to improve the trans-
parency of dataset ranking by providing interpretable explanations for retrieval decisions.
Unlike conventional keyword-based retrieval systems, the developed framework explicitly
communicates the factors contributing to each ranking decision through metadata-driven
explanations, relevance scores, and fuzzy reasoning.
As summarized in Table 7.2, participants responded positively to the explainability
features of the system. The questionnaire items evaluating explanation clarity (Mean =
4.60), transparency of ranking decisions (Mean = 4.50), and the usefulness of relevance
scores (Mean = 4.50) all received high levels of agreement with relatively small standard
deviations. These findings indicate that participants consistently perceived the generated
explanations as understandable and useful for interpreting ranking decisions.
Participants particularly appreciated that the explanation panel described ranking
decisions using familiar metadata attributes rather than internal model parameters. In-
stead of presenting technical information about the fuzzy inference process, the system
explained rankings using interpretable factors such as keyword matching, metadata com-
pleteness, publication recency, and resource availability. This design choice aligns with HCIR
principles by allowing users to understand why datasets were ranked highly without re-
quiring knowledge of the underlying ranking algorithm.
The comparatively lower score for the usefulness of metadata in supporting relevance
judgments (Mean = 4.10, SD = 0.88) suggests that explanation quality is partly depen-
dent on the completeness and consistency of metadata provided by the Swiss OGD portal.
Incomplete or inconsistent metadata may therefore reduce the effectiveness of explanation
generation, representing an important limitation of metadata-driven retrieval systems.
The evaluation indicates that the explanation mechanism successfully improved the
transparency of the proposed retrieval framework by making the ranking process more
understandable to participants. Unlike post-hoc explanation approaches that attempt to
approximate model behaviour after a ranking decision has been produced, the explana-
tions generated by the proposed HCFIR framework are derived directly from the fuzzy
inference process and therefore reflect the actual reasoning used to computethe final rank-
ing scores. Consequently, the explanations are intrinsically linked to the decision-making
process of the retrieval system rather than being independently generated interpretations.
Although the present evaluation focused on users’ perceived usefulness of the ex-
planations rather than formal measures of explanation fidelity, the results demonstrate
that integrating interpretable fuzzy reasoning with explicit rule-based explanations can
enhance transparency while maintaining competitive retrieval effectiveness. Future work
may investigate additional methods for quantitatively evaluating explanation faithfulness
and user understanding.

7.7 Contribution of Fuzzy Logic to HCIR
One of the principal objectives of this research was not to replace traditional IR mod-
els, but to investigate how fuzzy logic can enhance HCIR by incorporating multiple
interpretable relevance criteria into the ranking process. Conventional keyword-based re-
trieval methods, such as BM25, primarily rank documents according to lexical similarity,
whereas the proposed framework combines textual relevance with metadata completeness,
dataset recency, resource availability, and additional human-centered relevance factors
through a Mamdani fuzzy inference system.
The quantitative evaluation presented in Section 7.3 demonstrates that conventional
retrieval approaches achieved higher values for several standard IR metrics, including
MAP and nDCG@10. This outcome is expected because the benchmark relevance judg-
ments were largely derived from metadata-based matching, naturally favouring retrieval
approaches that optimize lexical similarity and metadata overlap. Nevertheless, the pro-
posed fuzzy framework maintained competitive retrieval effectiveness while providing
additional capabilities that are not reflected by traditional ranking metrics alone.
The qualitative evaluation further demonstrates the practical value of the fuzzy rank-
ing approach. As summarized in Table 7.2, participants consistently reported positive
perceptions regarding transparency, explanation quality, and trust in the retrieval results.
The explanation mechanism enabled users to understand why datasets were ranked highly
by presenting interpretable reasoning based on metadata quality, keyword relevance, re-
cency, and fuzzy rule activation rather than opaque numerical scores.
Unlike weighted-sum ranking approaches, the Mamdani fuzzy inference system models
gradual transitions between linguistic concepts such aslow,medium, andhighrelevance,

seeking. Furthermore, fuzzy rule-based reasoning remains fully interpretable because
each ranking decision can be traced back to explicitly defined membership functions and

interpretability [42, 9].
Overall, the evaluation suggests that the primary contribution of the proposed frame-
work lies not in maximizing conventional retrieval metrics, but in providing an inter-

transparency, explainability, and user trust. These characteristics are particularly impor-
tant for OGD portals, where users frequently perform exploratory searches and require
understandable explanations for retrieval decisions rather than only highly optimized
ranking scores.

7.9 Limitations
Although the proposed HCIR framework demonstrated encouraging results, several lim-
itations should be considered when interpreting the findings of this research.
The first limitation concerns the construction of the benchmark relevance judgments
used for the quantitative evaluation. The benchmark was reconstructed from the over-
lapping judgments that exist in the frozen corpus snapshot and therefore covers only a
small subset of the intended pooled collection. While this approach provides a practical
and reproducible evaluation framework, it naturally favours retrieval approaches that
retrieve the aligned judged items and it leaves some benchmark queries coverage-limited.
Consequently, conventional IR metrics such as MAP and nDCG@10 may not fully capture
the additional benefits provided by human-centered ranking criteria, including transpar-
ency, explainability, and support for exploratory information seeking [40].
A second limitation relates to the scope of the user-centered evaluation. The forma-
tive questionnaire involved ten Master?s students who were familiar with IR concepts and
digital technologies. Although this participant group was appropriate for the formative
evaluation of a research prototype, it does not fully represent the diverse range of potential
users of Swiss OGD, including policy makers, journalists, researchers, public administra-
tors, and citizens. Future studies should therefore involve a larger and more heterogeneous
participant population to improve the external validity of the findings [14, 32].
The evaluation was also limited to datasets retrieved from a single Swiss OGD portal
snapshot. Although the snapshot provides a realistic and representative environment for
the proposed framework, the reported findings may not directly generalize to other OGD
platforms that employ different metadata standards, retrieval infrastructures, or ranking
strategies. Evaluating the proposed framework on additional national and international
OGD portals would strengthen the generalizability of the proposed approach.
Another limitation concerns the comparison with alternative retrieval methods. The
benchmark focused on conventional keyword-based retrieval and weighted metadata rank-
ing approaches because these represent the retrieval mechanisms currently available within
many OGD portals. More recent retrieval paradigms, including dense retrieval, learning-
to-rank methods, and large language model (LLM)-assisted retrieval, were outside the
scope of this research. Future work could investigate how fuzzy HCIR can complement
these modern retrieval approaches while preserving transparency and explainability [9].
Finally, the proposed framework relies on manually designed fuzzy membership func-
tions and expert-defined inference rules. Although this design improves interpretability
and allows users to understand how ranking decisions are produced, it may require adap-
tation when applied to different application domains or metadata schemas. Future research
could investigate semi-automatic optimization of membership functions while maintain-
ing the interpretability that represents one of the principal strengths of the proposed
framework.
Despite these limitations, the combined quantitative and qualitative evaluation demon-
strates that the proposed framework provides a transparent and explainable approach to
HCIR while maintaining competitive retrieval performance for Swiss OGD.

parency, explainability, and user trust. Although conventional retrieval approaches achieved
higher values for several traditional IR metrics, participants consistently reported posi-
tive perceptions regarding usability, explanation quality, and confidence in the retrieval
results. These findings suggest that the principal contribution of the framework lies in
improving user understanding of ranking decisions while maintaining practical retrieval
effectiveness.
8.3.4 RQ4: How effective are metadata-driven explanations in
improving user understanding and trust in ranking deci-
sions?
The qualitative evaluation indicates that metadata-driven explanations substantially im-
proved participant understanding of retrieval decisions. Users consistently reported that
explanation messages, relevance scores, and metadata summaries enabled them to inter-
pret why datasets appeared in particular ranking positions and increased their confidence
in the retrieval results. Although explanation quality ultimately depends on the availabil-
ity and consistency of metadata, the findings demonstrate that interpretable explanation
mechanisms represent an important component of HCIR for OGD portals.
8.4 Research Contributions
This research contributes to the field of HCIR by demonstrating how fuzzy logic can
be integrated with conventional IR techniques to improve the discoverability and inter-
pretability of Swiss OGD. The principal contributions of this thesis are summarized as
follows.
1. A HCIR framework for Open Government Data.
This thesis proposes a HCIR framework that extends traditional keyword-based
retrieval by integrating multiple metadata-based relevance criteria within an in-
terpretable fuzzy inference process. Unlike conventional retrieval approaches that
primarily emphasize lexical similarity, the proposed framework explicitly consid-
ers transparency, explainability, metadata quality, and user understanding during
ranking.
2. A custom fuzzy multi-criteria ranking mechanism.
A Mamdani fuzzy inference engine was designed and implemented to combine key-
word relevance, metadata completeness, dataset recency, and resource availability
into a unified ranking score. The rule-based design allows gradual reasoning under
uncertainty while maintaining full interpretability of ranking decisions.
3. An explainable retrieval interface for Swiss Open Government Data.
The developed prototype integrates metadata-driven explanation generation di-
rectly into the retrieval interface. Rather than presenting opaque ranking scores,
the system explains retrieval decisions using interpretable factors such as keyword
matching, metadata completeness, publication recency, and resource availability,
thereby supporting user understanding and trust.
4. Integration with the live Swiss OGD portal.
Unlike many conceptual HCIR studies, the proposed framework was implemented as
a functional prototype connected to the live CKAN API of the Swiss OGD portal.
The system retrieves real datasets and demonstrates the practical feasibility of
transparent and explainable retrieval within an operational OGD environment.

8.5 Theoretical Implications
Beyond the implementation of a functional retrieval prototype, this research contributes
to the theoretical understanding of HCIR by demonstrating that retrieval effectiveness
alone is insufficient for evaluating exploratory OGD search systems. The findings indicate
that transparency, explainability, and user trust represent complementary dimensions of
retrieval quality that should be considered alongside traditional IR performance metrics.
The proposed framework further demonstrates that fuzzy logic provides a practical
and interpretable mechanism for modelling the uncertainty that naturally occurs during
exploratory information seeking. Rather than relying exclusively on precise numerical
relevance scores, the Mamdani fuzzy inference system enables gradual reasoning using
linguistic concepts such aslow,medium, andhighrelevance. This approach aligns more
closely with the way users evaluate the usefulness of datasets during exploratory search,
where relevance is often subjective rather than binary.
The evaluation also highlights the importance of integrating technical and human-
centered evaluation methods within DSR. While quantitative IR metrics provide objec-
tive measures of retrieval performance, the qualitative user study demonstrates that user
perceptions of transparency, explanation quality, and trust represent equally important
indicators of system effectiveness. The combination of both evaluation approaches there-
fore provides a more comprehensive understanding of retrieval quality than either method
alone.

trust the retrieval process. These findings contribute to the growing body of research
advocating transparent and user-centered approaches to IR and decision support systems

8.6 Practical Implications
The outcomes of this research demonstrate that HCIR can provide practical benefits
for the design and operation of OGD portals by improving the transparency and in-
terpretability of dataset retrieval. While conventional keyword-based retrieval systems
remain effective for identifying relevant datasets, the proposed framework illustrates how
explainable multi-criteria ranking can improve the overall search experience without re-
placing existing retrieval infrastructures.
For OGD providers, the proposed framework offers a practical approach for enhancing
dataset discoverability while preserving compatibility with existing CKAN-based search
platforms. Since the fuzzy inference engine operates as an additional ranking layer, the
framework can be incorporated into existing retrieval systems with relatively limited
architectural changes. The explanation mechanism further enables users to understand
why datasets appear in particular ranking positions, thereby increasing confidence in
retrieval results and encouraging more effective exploration of public datasets.
The findings also have practical implications for government organizations responsible
for publishing OGD. The evaluation highlights the importance of high-quality metadata,
including accurate descriptions, meaningful keywords, complete resource information,
and up-to-date publication dates. Improvements in metadata quality not only enhance
conventional retrieval methods but also strengthen explanation generation and multi-
criteria ranking within human-centered retrieval systems.
From a broader perspective, the research demonstrates that retrieval systems should
be evaluated using both technical performance measures and user-centered criteria. The
combination of quantitative IR benchmarking with qualitative user evaluation provides
a more comprehensive assessment of retrieval quality than either approach alone. This
evaluation strategy may be valuable for future HCIR research and for the development
of transparent decision-support systems in other application domains.
Finally, the proposed framework provides a practical foundation for future retrieval
systems that seek to balance retrieval effectiveness with transparency, explainability, and
user trust. These characteristics are becoming increasingly important as public-sector
information systems continue to evolve toward more user-oriented and explainable digital

8.7 Limitations
Although the proposed HCIR framework achieved the objectives of this research, the
findings should be interpreted within the scope of the evaluation conducted in this study.
First, the quantitative evaluation was performed using a benchmark developed for
the Swiss OGD portal. While the benchmark provided a consistent basis for comparing
different retrieval approaches, the relevance judgments were derived from metadata-based
annotations and subsequently reviewed manually. Consequently, the reported IR metrics
primarily evaluate ranking effectiveness with respect to lexical and metadata similarity
and may not fully capture human-centered characteristics such as transparency, explain-
ability, and user understanding.
Second, the qualitative evaluation involved ten Master’s students who participated in
a controlled user study. Although this participant group was appropriate for evaluating a
research prototype and provided valuable insights into user interaction with the proposed
framework, the findings should not be interpreted as representing the broader population
of OGD users. Additional studies involving larger and more diverse participant groups
would further strengthen the external validity of the results.
Third, the framework was evaluated exclusively using datasets retrieved from the
Swiss OGD portal. While this provided a realistic application environment, differences
in metadata quality, portal architecture, and retrieval infrastructure may influence the
performance of the proposed approach when applied to other OGD platforms.
These limitations do not diminish the overall contribution of the research. Instead,
theydefinethescopewithinwhichthereportedfindingsshouldbeinterpretedandidentify
opportunities for extending the proposed framework in future studies.
8.8 Future Work
The findings of this research provide several opportunities for extending the proposed
HCIR framework and further advancing research on transparent and explainable retrieval
for OGD.
A natural direction for future work is the evaluation of the proposed framework with a
larger and more diverse participant population. The current user study provided valuable
formative insights into usability, transparency, and trust; however, involving government
agencies, policy makers, researchers, journalists, and citizens would provide a broader un-
derstanding of user requirements and improve the generalizability of the findings. Larger
participant groups would also enable more comprehensive statistical analyses of user be-
haviour and system acceptance.

--- PAGE 99 ---
Another important direction involves evaluating the framework across multiple OGD
portals. The present study focused exclusively on the Swiss OGD portal because of its
comprehensive metadata and open CKAN-based infrastructure. Applying the frame-
work to additional national and international OGD portals would allow the robustness
of the proposed ranking methodology to be assessed across different metadata standards,
retrieval infrastructures, and application domains [25].
Future research may also investigate the integration of modern IR techniques with
the proposed HCIR framework. Dense retrieval models, learning-to-rank approaches, and
Large Language Model (LLM)-assisted retrieval have demonstrated promising retrieval
performance in recent years. Rather than replacing the proposed fuzzy inference system,
these techniques could be incorporated as complementary retrieval components while
preserving the transparency and explainability that represent the principal strengths of
the proposed framework.
The fuzzy inference engine itself offers several opportunities for further development.
Future studies could investigate adaptive optimization of membership functions and fuzzy
rules using user interaction data or machine learning techniques while maintaining the
interpretability of the ranking process. Such an approach could improve retrieval perfor-
mance without sacrificing the transparency that distinguishes fuzzy rule-based reasoning
from many black-box learning methods.
Another promising direction involves introducing personalized HCIR. The current
framework applies a common ranking strategy for all users, whereas future systems could
adapt ranking preferences according to user expertise, search objectives, domain knowl-
edge, or previous search behaviour. Combining personalization with transparent expla-
nation mechanisms would allow users to understand not only why datasets were retrieved
but also how their individual preferences influenced ranking decisions.
Finally, future work could further enhance the explanation component by generating
richer natural-language explanations that combine metadata analysis, fuzzy reasoning,
and user context. Such explanations could improve user understanding while support-
ing greater confidence in retrieval decisions, particularly during exploratory information
seeking and decision-support tasks [40, 26].
Overall, the proposed framework establishes a strong foundation for future HCIR
research by demonstrating that transparent, explainable, and user-oriented retrieval can
successfully complement conventional IR techniques within OGD environments.
8.9 Final Remarks
This thesis set out to investigate how HCIR can improve access to Swiss OGD through
the integration of fuzzy logic-based multi-criteria ranking and explainable retrieval mech-
anisms. The research has demonstrated that effective IR is not solely determined by

ranking accuracy, but also by the extent to which users can understand, interpret, and
trust the retrieval process.
The proposed framework combines conventional IR techniques with an interpretable
Mamdani fuzzy inference system and metadata-driven explanation generation to sup-
port transparent and user-centered dataset discovery. Through its implementation and
evaluation, the research demonstrates that fuzzy logic can successfully complement tra-
ditional keyword-based retrieval by incorporating multiple human-centered relevance cri-

Beyond the technical implementation, this research contributes to the broader ob-

encourages greater confidence in OGD systems.

improving user understanding of ranking decisions, and promoting greater transparency
in dataset discovery. Although the evaluation was conducted on a limited corpus and
participant sample, the results indicate that transparent ranking approaches can provide
meaningful benefits beyond conventional retrieval effectiveness metrics. The proposed
framework therefore establishes a practical and reproducible foundation for future re-
search on explainable and human-centered retrieval systems within OGD environments.
The findings of this research contribute to the growing body of work on transparent
and explainable IR by demonstrating that effective retrieval systems should be evalu-
ated not only by their ranking performance but also by their ability to support human
understanding, trust, and informed decision-making.
